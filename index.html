F1-Analytics

A complete lakehouse on Databricks: 77 seasons of Formula 1 data, loaded incrementally, modeled for analysis, and used to forecast the 2026 championship.

View code on GitHub

Why I built it

At NatWest I worked on pieces of a large data platform, but never got to build one end to end on a modern lakehouse stack. Formula 1 is a great dataset for that: every race since 1950, with drivers, constructors, results and standings that all relate to each other in ways that reward good modeling.

The goal was to treat it like a production system rather than a notebook: governed storage, repeatable loads, scheduled jobs, and a clean analytical layer that anyone could query.

How it works

  • Ergast API
  • →
  • Bronze: raw
  • →
  • Silver: cleaned
  • →
  • Gold: models and views
  • →
  • Dashboard and forecast

Data lands from the Ergast API into a bronze layer exactly as received, so there is always an untouched copy to reprocess from. The silver layer cleans and conforms it, and the gold layer holds the data models analysts actually use. Everything is stored in Delta Lake, governed through Unity Catalog, and orchestrated with Lakeflow Jobs.

Loading is incremental: when a new race weekend arrives, only the new results are processed, instead of rebuilding 77 seasons of history every time. That's the difference between a pipeline you run once and one you can leave running all season.

On top of the gold layer sit six analytical views, covering driver standings, constructor standings, and teammate head-to-head comparisons, which power an AI/BI dashboard.

The 2026 forecast

The last piece is a scoring model that predicts the 2026 championship order. It looks at the past five seasons of driver and constructor results and weights them with exponential decay, so last year counts far more than five years ago. Recent form is a much better guide than career totals.

New teams create a gap: they have no history to score. Rather than giving them zero, which would rank them unfairly last, the model gives them a median baseline.

What I'd flag honestly

2026 brought one of the biggest regulation changes in years, which resets a lot of the competitive order. So even recent form is a weaker predictor this season than usual. The forecast is a reasoned starting point, not a crystal ball, and comparing it against the real results as the season goes on is half the fun.

HealthShield

Two health insurance problems on one reproducible pipeline: which providers are worth investigating for fraud, and how much will a group of members cost?

Try the live app View code on GitHub

The problem

Insurers lose money two ways: to fraudulent claims, and to costs they didn't plan for. Fraud investigators can only look at so many providers, so the useful question isn't "is this provider fraudulent?" but "if we can only check a handful, which ones first?" And for cost, finance teams need to budget for groups of members, not guess at individuals.

I worked on both, using 558,000 Medicare claims from 5,410 providers and records for 100,000 members.

How it works

  • Raw claims and members
  • →
  • Bronze
  • →
  • Silver + quality tests
  • →
  • Gold features
  • →
  • Models
  • →
  • Streamlit dashboard

The pipeline follows the same bronze, silver and gold layering I'd use at work, with unit tests checking data quality as it moves between layers. A single command rebuilds everything from raw data to final metrics, so anyone can reproduce the results.

The Streamlit dashboard reads its numbers straight from the files the pipeline writes. Nothing is typed in by hand, so the dashboard can never drift from what the models actually produced.

Guardrails that caught real problems

I built leakage and fairness audits into the pipeline, and they earned their place. One feature turned out to predict the cost target almost perfectly, with an R² of 0.932, because it was effectively derived from the answer. The audit caught it before it reached modeling. Another check found a strong size-related bias in the fraud labels: larger providers were far more likely to be labeled fraudulent, which a naive model would happily learn as a shortcut.

Results, and how I tested them

For fraud, I compared the model against a simple baseline that just flags the providers billing the most. If a model can't beat that, it isn't adding anything. I also fixed the decision threshold before looking at test results, so I couldn't tune my way to a better number.

  • Among the top 5% of providers the model flags, 84% are fraudulent, compared with 9.4% if you picked at random.
  • It beats the billing-volume baseline by 0.073 PR-AUC, with a 95% confidence interval to show the gain isn't luck.
  • The cost model estimates total cost for a 20,000-member group to within 0.79%.

What the cost model can't do

For a single member, the cost model's typical error is 87% of the median member's cost. That sounds bad, and for pricing an individual policy it would be. But individual healthcare costs are hugely unpredictable, and those errors cancel out across a group. So the honest conclusion is that this model is a budgeting tool, not a pricing tool, and I'd rather say that clearly than hide it.

ClariCase

A consumer writes a complaint in their own words. ClariCase reads it, sends it to the right support team, and gives them a tracking ID.

Try the live app View code on GitHub

The problem

When someone complains to a bank about a credit report error or a mortgage problem, the complaint usually lands in a general queue and waits for a person to read it and pass it on. Every hop adds days. With 11 possible teams, from Credit Reporting to Mortgage, getting it right the first time matters.

ClariCase is built on real complaints filed with the U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau: about 1.9 GB of complaints from 2024 and 2025, written by real people in plain, messy language.

How it works

  • Complaint text
  • →
  • TF-IDF features
  • →
  • Calibrated classifier
  • →
  • Confident? Auto-route
  • →
  • Unsure? Human review

The model turns complaint text into TF-IDF features and classifies it with a calibrated Naive Bayes model. Calibration means a 90% confidence score really is right about 90% of the time, which makes it possible to set a confidence bar: complaints above it are routed automatically, and everything else goes to a person.

The app runs on Streamlit, stores complaints and their tracking IDs in Supabase, and falls back to a local SQLite database if the connection drops.

Decisions that mattered

  • Test on the future, not a random sample. The model was trained on earlier complaints and tested on 109,834 complaints from October to December 2025. That mirrors real use, where the model always sees complaints newer than its training data.
  • Precision over coverage. A wrongly routed complaint is worse than one that waits for a human. So the main measure is how many complaints can be routed automatically while staying very accurate, not raw accuracy across everything.
  • Learn from all banks. An earlier baseline was trained only on JPMorgan Chase complaints. Training on the full multi-bank data roughly doubled what could be routed safely.

Results

ClariCase routes 46.5% of complaints automatically at 94.4% precision, up from 26.4% with the earlier baseline. Across all complaints, overall accuracy is 0.800 with a macro F1 of 0.647. The project is still in active development.

ChurnWave

Which customers are about to leave, and what should the retention team do about each one? An app that answers both.

Try the live app View code on GitHub

The problem

For a telecom company, keeping a customer is far cheaper than winning a new one. But a churn score on its own doesn't help much. A retention team needs to know who to call first and what to offer, and they need it for a whole customer list at once, not one record at a time.

How it works

  • Customer data
  • →
  • 4-model ensemble
  • →
  • Risk tier
  • →
  • Retention action
  • →
  • Export

I trained four models on 7,043 telecom customer records and combined them into an ensemble. The best model reaches a ROC-AUC of 0.887, meaning it ranks a customer who will leave above one who won't about 89% of the time.

The app turns those scores into something a team can act on. Each customer is placed in a risk tier, and each tier comes with a retention recommendation. You can score one customer by hand, or upload a whole CSV or Excel file, score everyone at once, and export the results.

Why risk tiers instead of raw scores

A probability like 0.73 means little to someone running a retention campaign. Tiers translate the model into decisions: who gets a call this week, who gets an offer, and who is fine for now. That small design choice is what makes the model usable by people who will never look at a ROC curve.

Music and Mental Health

Music calms me down. But is that true for everyone, and does it depend on what you listen to, or how?

Open the Tableau dashboard Try the live app View code on GitHub

Where the question came from

For me, music is how I calm down. But I knew that isn't always the case, for me or for anyone else.

So I wanted to look past the usual "music is good for you" and ask a more specific question: how do different kinds of music, and different ways of engaging with it, relate to people's mental health?

What I looked at

The data is a survey where people reported their listening habits, including hours per day, favorite genres, whether they listen while working, and whether they play an instrument, compose, or explore new music. They also rated their own anxiety and depression, and said whether music improves or worsens how they feel.

  • Survey responses
  • →
  • Cleaning in Python
  • →
  • Statistical analysis and modeling
  • →
  • Tableau dashboard and Streamlit app

What I found

  • The people music hurts are already struggling more. Respondents who said music worsens their mental health had significantly higher anxiety and depression scores than those who said it helps.
  • How you engage matters more than how much. Playing an instrument, composing, or exploring new genres was a stronger predictor of feeling that music helps than total listening time.
  • More hours don't mean better mental health. Listening longer, on its own, showed no improvement. Context did: whether you listen while working, which genres you choose, and how actively you engage.

That third finding matched my own experience better than I expected. Music helps me when I choose it on purpose, not when it's just running in the background.

A fair caveat

This is self-reported survey data, so these are associations, not causes. People who feel worse may simply experience music differently, rather than music making them feel worse. The dashboard is built so you can explore the patterns yourself and draw your own conclusions.
